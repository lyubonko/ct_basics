1.1 Categories

Reading Basis: Paolo Perrone, Notes on Category Theory with examples from basic mathematics, Chapter 1: Basic concepts, Section 1.1 Categories (pp. 8–23).

1. Rigorous Definition of a Category (Definition 1.1.1)

Definition 1.1.1. A category \(\mathsf{C}\) consists of:

  • A collection \(\mathsf{C}_0\), whose elements are called the objects of \(\mathsf{C}\) and are usually denoted by uppercase letters \(X, Y, Z, \dots\)
  • A collection \(\mathsf{C}_1\), whose elements are called the morphisms or arrows of \(\mathsf{C}\) and are usually denoted by lowercase letters \(f, g, h, \dots\)

such that:

  • Source and Target (Domain and Codomain): Each morphism \(f\) has assigned two objects, called source and target (or domain and codomain), denoted by \(s(f)\) and \(t(f)\), respectively. If \(s(f) = X\) and \(t(f) = Y\), we write \[f : X \to Y \qquad \text{or more graphically} \qquad X \xrightarrow{\;f\;} Y.\]
  • Identity Morphism: Each object \(X\) has a distinguished morphism \[\mathrm{id}_X : X \to X,\] called the identity morphism of \(X\) (with \(s(\mathrm{id}_X) = t(\mathrm{id}_X) = X\)).
  • Composition: For each pair of morphisms \(f, g\) such that \(t(f) = s(g)\), there exists a specified morphism \(g \circ f\), called the composite morphism, such that \(s(g \circ f) = s(f)\) and \(t(g \circ f) = t(g)\). More graphically: \[f : X \to Y, \quad g : Y \to Z \qquad \leadsto \qquad g \circ f : X \to Z.\]

These structures must satisfy two axioms:

  1. Unitality: For every morphism \(f : X \to Y\), the compositions \(f \circ \mathrm{id}_X\) and \(\mathrm{id}_Y \circ f\) are both equal to \(f\): \[f \circ \mathrm{id}_X = f = \mathrm{id}_Y \circ f.\]
  2. Associativity: For \(f : X \to Y\), \(g : Y \to Z\), and \(h : Z \to W\), the compositions \(h \circ (g \circ f)\) and \((h \circ g) \circ f\) are equal: \[h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f.\]

Caveat: Reading Order of Composition ("\(g\) after \(f\)")

The composite \(g \circ f\) means first applying \(f\), then \(g\). This choice of order—which writes \(g\) to the left of \(f\) even though the diagram flows left-to-right \(X \xrightarrow{f} Y \xrightarrow{g} Z\)—comes from traditional functional notation such as \(\sin(\cos(x))\), where one first takes the cosine and then the sine.

Tip: To avoid confusion when reading formulas alongside diagrams, always read \(g \circ f\) aloud as "\(g\) after \(f\)".

Step-by-Step Depiction of Associativity

Given three composable morphisms \(X \xrightarrow{f} Y \xrightarrow{g} Z \xrightarrow{h} W\), there are a priori two ways to form the triple composite:

  • Method 1 (Upper/First Pair): First compose \(f\) and \(g\) to obtain \(g \circ f : X \to Z\), and then compose with \(h\) to obtain \(h \circ (g \circ f) : X \to W\).
  • Method 2 (Lower/Second Pair): First compose \(g\) and \(h\) to obtain \(h \circ g : Y \to W\), and then compose \(f\) with \(h \circ g\) to obtain \((h \circ g) \circ f : X \to W\).

2. First Perspective: Categories as Relations (§1.1.1)

Idea 1.1.1. A category is a collection of objects which are related to each other in a consistent way.

In all examples of this type, given two objects \(x, y \in X\), there is at most one morphism \(x \to y\) between them:

Relation Type on \(X\)Defining AxiomsCategorical Interpretation (\(x \to y \iff x R y\))
Preorder \((X, \lesssim)\) (Example 1.1.4)(a) Reflexivity: \(x \lesssim x\)
(b) Transitivity: \(x \lesssim y \land y \lesssim z \implies x \lesssim z\)		Most general relation forming a category.
• Reflexivity \(\leadsto\) identity \(\mathrm{id}_x : x \to x\)
• Transitivity \(\leadsto\) composition \((y \to z) \circ (x \to y) = (x \to z)\)
• Unitality & associativity hold automatically by uniqueness of arrows!
Partial Order / Poset \((X, \le)\) (Example 1.1.3)Preorder +
(c) Antisymmetry: \(x \le y \land y \le x \implies x = y\)		Preorder category where no two distinct objects can have arrows in both directions (skeletal: isomorphic objects are equal).
Equivalence Relation \((X, \sim)\) (Example 1.1.2)Preorder +
(c) Symmetry: \(x \sim y \implies y \sim x\)		Preorder category where every arrow is invertible ("we can always go back" — a groupoid). Partitions \(X\) into connected clusters (equivalence classes).

Concrete Examples from §1.1.1

  • Example 1.1.2 ("Has the same shape as"): On a set of geometric shapes \(\{\circ, \bigcirc, \bullet, \blacksquare, \square, \triangle\}\), drawing an arrow \(x \to y\) whenever "\(x\) has the same shape as \(y\)" (regardless of size or color) creates three connected components: a complete bidirectional triangle on the three circles \(\{\circ, \bigcirc, \bullet\}\), a bidirectional pair on the two squares \(\{\blacksquare, \square\}\), and an isolated loop on the triangle \(\{\triangle\}\).
  • Example 1.1.4 (Price in Economics as a Preorder): Let \(x \lesssim y\) mean "\(x\) is cheaper or equally priced to \(y\)."
  • Reflexivity: \(x\) has lower or equal price to itself.
  • Transitivity: if \(x\) is no more expensive than \(y\), and \(y\) is no more expensive than \(z\), then \(x\) is no more expensive than \(z\).
  • Why it is a Preorder and NOT a Partial Order: If \(x \lesssim y\) and \(y \lesssim x\), \(x\) and \(y\) do not have to be the exact same object (\(x = y\))—they simply have the same price! Categorically, they are isomorphic (you can trade \(x\) for \(y\) and vice versa without losing value).

3. Second Perspective: Categories as Operations (§1.1.2)

Idea 1.1.2. A category is a collection of operations which can be composed in a consistent way.

What if we have a collection of operations (like symmetries or transformations of a system) where any two operations can be composed without needing to match different sources and targets? To view such a structure as a category, we make all morphisms share a single formal object \(\bullet\).

Groups and Their Delooping \(\mathsf{B}G\) (Example 1.1.6 & Definition 1.1.7)

  • Group \(G\) (Example 1.1.6): A nonempty set \(G\) with a unit \(1 \in G\), multiplication \((g, h) \mapsto g \cdot h\), and inverses \(g^{-1} \in G\) satisfying:
  • Unitality: \(g \cdot 1 = 1 \cdot g = g\);
  • Associativity: \((g \cdot h) \cdot i = g \cdot (h \cdot i)\);
  • Inverse law: \(g \cdot g^{-1} = g^{-1} \cdot g = 1\).
  • Delooping \(\mathsf{B}G\) (Definition 1.1.7): Let \(G\) be a group. The delooping of \(G\), denoted \(\mathsf{B}G\), is the category with:
  • A single object \(\bullet\);
  • A morphism \(g : \bullet \to \bullet\) for each element \(g \in G\);
  • The identity \(\mathrm{id}_\bullet\) given by the neutral element \(1 \in G\);
  • The composition \(g \circ h\) of \(g, h : \bullet \to \bullet\) given by group multiplication \(g \cdot h \in G\).
  • Remarks 1.1.8 & 1.1.9: \(\mathsf{B}G\) encodes the exact same data as \(G\). While formally \(\bullet\) is an abstract point, when \(G\) models symmetries of a concrete space (such as rotations of \(\mathbb{R}^2\)), we can concretely picture \(\bullet\) as \(\mathbb{R}^2\) and the loops as linear rotations \(\mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2\).

Monoids and \(\mathsf{B}M\): Dropping Inverses (Example 1.1.10)

Notice that constructing \(\mathsf{B}G\) never used inverses \(g^{-1}\)! Dropping the inverse axiom gives a monoid \((M, \cdot, 1)\): a set with an associative binary operation and a two-sided unit \(1 \in M\). Its delooping \(\mathsf{B}M\) is again a one-object category.

Whereas a group models reversible symmetries, a monoid models transformations that are not necessarily invertible:

  • All linear maps \(V \to V\) on a vector space \(V\) (including the zero map \((x, y) \mapsto (0, 0)\) on \(\mathbb{R}^2\)).
  • All continuous maps \(X \to X\) on a topological space \(X\).
  • All functions \(X \to X\) on a set \(X\).
  • Probability Theory (Markov Semigroups): Given a measurable space \(X\) and a Markov transition kernel \(k : X \to X\), the powers \[\{\mathrm{id}_X, k, k^2, k^3, \dots\}\] form a monoid (often called the Markov semigroup generated by \(k\)), and all Markov kernels \(X \to X\) form the full Markov semigroup on \(X\) (Remark 1.1.11 notes that category theory always includes the identity \(\mathrm{id}_X\), "doing nothing").

4. Third Perspective: Spaces and Maps with Extra Structure (§1.1.3)

Idea 1.1.3. A category is a collection of sets or spaces equipped with extra structure, and maps between them which are compatible with that structure.

  • In §1.1.1 (Relations), we never had to check equality of parallel composites because there was at most one arrow between any two objects.
  • In §1.1.2 (Operations), we never had to check whether sources and targets matched because there was only one object.
  • In §1.1.3 (General Categories of Structured Spaces), we have many objects and many morphisms between them, so we must always check both domain/codomain matching and composition closure/axioms!

Standard Categories of Structured Spaces (Examples 1.1.13–1.1.15)

\(\mathsf{Set}\)SetsFunctions \(f : X \to Y\)
\(\mathsf{Top}\)Topological spacesContinuous maps
\(\mathsf{Mfd}\)Smooth manifoldsSmooth (\(C^\infty\)) maps
\(\mathsf{Vect}\)Vector spacesLinear maps

\(\mathsf{Poset}\)Partially ordered setsMonotone maps: \(x \le x' \implies f(x) \le f(x')\)

Crucial Non-Example (Example 1.1.17): Why Convex Functions Do NOT Form a Category!

Whenever you propose a category, you must verify that the composite of two valid morphisms is still a valid morphism.

Consider trying to form a category with object \(\mathbb{R}\) and convex functions \(\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) as morphisms. Take: \[f(x) := x^2 \qquad \text{and} \qquad g(x) := x^2 - 1.\] Both \(f\) and \(g\) are strictly convex parabolas. However, their composite \(f \circ g : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) is: \[(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x)) = (x^2 - 1)^2 = x^4 - 2x^2 + 1.\] Evaluating at \(x = -1, 0, +1\): \[(f \circ g)(-1) = 0, \qquad (f \circ g)(0) = 1, \qquad (f \circ g)(1) = 0.\] Because the midpoint value \((f \circ g)(0) = 1\) lies above the average \(\frac{1}{2}(0 + 0) = 0\), the composite \(f \circ g\) has a "W" shape with a local maximum at \(x = 0\) and is not convex! Thus, convex functions fail closure under composition and do not form a category. (See Interactive Lab A below to explore this visually!)

5. Set-Theoretical Considerations & Hom-Sets (§1.1.4)

Why did Definition 1.1.1 speak of collections \(\mathsf{C}_0, \mathsf{C}_1\) rather than sets? Because of Russell's Paradox: there is no "set of all sets," so in the category \(\mathsf{Set}\) of all sets, the collection of objects \(\mathsf{Set}_0\) is a proper class (or a large set in a Grothendieck universe).

Definition 1.1.20 (Small and Locally Small Categories).

  • A category \(\mathsf{C}\) is called small if both \(\mathsf{C}_0\) and \(\mathsf{C}_1\) are sets.
  • A category \(\mathsf{C}\) is called locally small if for every pair of objects \(X\) and \(Y\) of \(\mathsf{C}\), the morphisms \(X \to Y\) form a set.

Definition 1.1.23 (Hom-Set). Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be objects of a locally small category \(\mathsf{C}\). The hom-set (or hom-space) of \(X\) and \(Y\), denoted \[\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathsf{C}}(X, Y),\] is the set of all morphisms in \(\mathsf{C}\) from \(X\) to \(Y\).

Caveat: Always keep track of the subscript \(\mathsf{C}\)! The same symbols \(\mathbb{R}\) and \(\mathbb{R}^2\) denote objects in many different categories, with drastically different hom-sets:
- \(\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathsf{Set}}(\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{R}^2)\) = the set of all arbitrary functions \(\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^2\).
- \(\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathsf{Top}}(\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{R}^2)\) = the set of all continuous functions \(\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^2\).
- \(\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathsf{Vect}}(\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{R}^2)\) = the set of all linear maps \(\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^2\) (a 2-dimensional space!).

6. Isomorphisms, Groupoids, and the Core (§1.1.5)

In equivalence relations (§1.1.1) and deloopings of groups (§1.1.2), "we can always go back." In a general category, this property is captured by isomorphisms.

Definition 1.1.24 (Isomorphism). Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be objects in a category \(\mathsf{C}\). An isomorphism is a pair of morphisms \[X \underset{g}{\overset{f}{\rightleftarrows}} Y\] such that: \[\text{(a)}\;\; g \circ f = \mathrm{id}_X, \qquad \text{and} \qquad \text{(b)}\;\; f \circ g = \mathrm{id}_Y.\] If an isomorphism between \(X\) and \(Y\) exists, we say \(X\) and \(Y\) are isomorphic (\(X \cong Y\)), and we write \(g = f^{-1}\) (which is unique by Exercise 1.1.26).

Caveat (Example 1.1.25): One-Sided Inverses Do NOT Imply Isomorphism!

Both conditions (a) and (b) must be checked separately! Let \(X = \mathbb{R}^2\) and \(Y = \mathbb{R}\):

  • Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}\) be the projection onto the \(x\)-axis: \(f(x, y) := x\).
  • Let \(g : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^2\) be the inclusion of the \(x\)-axis: \(g(x) := (x, 0)\).

Then for every \(x \in \mathbb{R}\), \[(f \circ g)(x) = f(x, 0) = x = \mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{R}}(x) \quad \implies \quad f \circ g = \mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{R}},\] but in the other order, for \((x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2\) with \(y \neq 0\), \[(g \circ f)(x, y) = g(x) = (x, 0) \neq (x, y) \quad \implies \quad g \circ f \neq \mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{R}^2}!\]

Isomorphisms in Familiar Categories (Example 1.1.27)

  • In \(\mathsf{Set}\): bijective functions.
  • In \(\mathsf{Top}\): homeomorphisms (continuous bijections whose inverse is also continuous—strictly stronger than a continuous bijection, e.g. \([0, 2\pi) \to S^1, t \mapsto (\cos t, \sin t)\) is a continuous bijection but not a homeomorphism!).
  • In \(\mathsf{Mfd}\): diffeomorphisms.
  • In \(\mathsf{Vect}\): invertible (bijective) linear maps.

Groupoids and the Core of a Category (Definitions 1.1.29 & 1.1.32)

  • Definition 1.1.29 (Groupoid): A groupoid is a category in which every morphism is invertible (an isomorphism).
  • Example 1.1.30: Any equivalence relation \((X, \sim)\) is a groupoid.
  • Example 1.1.31: The delooping \(\mathsf{B}G\) of any group \(G\) is a one-object groupoid.
  • Definition 1.1.32 (The Core of a Category): Given any category \(\mathsf{C}\), the core of \(\mathsf{C}\), denoted \(\mathsf{Core}(\mathsf{C})\), is the groupoid whose objects are the objects of \(\mathsf{C}\) and whose morphisms are only the isomorphisms of \(\mathsf{C}\).
  • Connection to Preorders: Taking the core of a preorder \((X, \lesssim)\) throws away the strictly one-way arrows and keeps only pairs where \(x \lesssim y\) and \(y \lesssim x\)—producing the exact equivalence relation \(x \sim y\) of Exercise 1.1.5!

Philosophical Remark (Equality vs. Isomorphism):
In ordinary category theory, we never ask whether two objects are equal, only whether they are isomorphic (just as any 3-dimensional real vector space is isomorphic to \(\mathbb{R}^3\), regardless of how its elements are constructed). By contrast, morphisms with the same source and target are compared by equality (\(g \circ f = \mathrm{id}_X\)). In higher category theory (\(2\)-categories, \(\infty\)-categories), even equalities between morphisms are replaced by higher isomorphisms (\(2\)-morphisms)!

7. Diagrams, Commutativity, and Pasting (§1.1.6)

Definition 1.1.33 (Diagram, informal). A diagram in a category \(\mathsf{C}\) is a directed (multi-)graph whose vertices are labeled by objects of \(\mathsf{C}\) and whose edges are labeled by morphisms of \(\mathsf{C}\), such that:

  1. Objects and morphisms may appear more than once as distinct vertices/edges;
  2. Multiple arrows may exist between any two vertices;
  3. For each vertex \(X\), the identity \(\mathrm{id}_X\) is implicitly present (even if not drawn);
  4. For every head-to-tail chain of edges \(X \xrightarrow{f} Y \xrightarrow{g} Z\), the composite \(g \circ f : X \to Z\) is implicitly present (even if not drawn).

Definition 1.1.35 (Commutative Diagram). A diagram is commutative (or commutes) if for every pair of vertices \(X\) and \(Y\) in the diagram, all compositions along directed paths of arrows connecting \(X\) to \(Y\) are equal.

Caveat: Not every diagram commutes! A diagram with parallel paths only commutes when we explicitly assert or prove that the composites along those paths are equal.

Diagram Pasting (Exercise 1.1.39)

One of the most powerful techniques in category theory is pasting commutative diagrams: if two adjacent squares commute separately, then the outer rectangle formed by pasting them along their shared edge automatically commutes!

\[ \left.\begin{aligned} \text{Left square commutes:} &\quad h \circ f = p \circ k \\ \text{Right square commutes:} &\quad \ell \circ g = q \circ h \end{aligned}\right\} \implies \ell \circ g \circ f = \ell \circ (h \circ f) = (\ell \circ h) \circ f = (q \circ h) \circ f = q \circ (h \circ f) = q \circ p \circ k. \]

8. The Opposite Category \(\mathsf{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\) and Duality (§1.1.7)

Another fundamental technique in category theory is "reversing all the arrows."

Definition 1.1.41 (The Opposite Category \(\mathsf{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\)). Let \(\mathsf{C}\) be a category. The opposite category of \(\mathsf{C}\), denoted \(\mathsf{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\), is defined by:

  • Objects: The exact same objects as \(\mathsf{C}\) (\(\mathrm{Ob}(\mathsf{C}^{\mathrm{op}}) = \mathrm{Ob}(\mathsf{C})\)).
  • Morphisms: A morphism \(f^{\mathrm{op}} : X \to Y\) in \(\mathsf{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\) is a morphism \(f : Y \to X\) in \(\mathsf{C}\) (mind the reversed direction!): \[\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathsf{C}^{\mathrm{op}}}(X, Y) := \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathsf{C}}(Y, X).\]
  • Identities: \(\mathrm{id}_X^{\mathrm{op}} := \mathrm{id}_X\).
  • Composition: Reversed order! Given \(X \xrightarrow{f^{\mathrm{op}}} Y \xrightarrow{g^{\mathrm{op}}} Z\) in \(\mathsf{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\) (which correspond to \(Z \xrightarrow{g} Y \xrightarrow{f} X\) in \(\mathsf{C}\)), their composite in \(\mathsf{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\) is: \[g^{\mathrm{op}} \circ f^{\mathrm{op}} := (f \circ g)^{\mathrm{op}}.\]

Examples of Opposite Categories

  • Example 1.1.42 (Posets): If \((X, \le)\) is a partially ordered set, then \((X, \le)^{\mathrm{op}} = (X, \ge)\) (ordered downward, e.g. \((\mathbb{R}, \le)^{\mathrm{op}} = (\mathbb{R}, \ge)\)).
  • Example 1.1.43 (Delooping of a Group): \((\mathsf{B}G)^{\mathrm{op}}\) is a one-object category whose morphisms are elements of \(G\) with multiplication performed in the opposite order: \(g^{\mathrm{op}} \circ h^{\mathrm{op}} = (h \cdot g)^{\mathrm{op}}\) (the opposite group \(G^{\mathrm{op}}\), which is isomorphic to \(G\) via \(g \mapsto g^{-1}\)).

The Principle of Duality (Corollaries 1.1.45 & 1.1.46)

Because the axioms of a category (unitality and associativity) are completely symmetric under reversing arrows:

Duality Principle: A categorical statement holds in every category \(\mathsf{C}\) if and only if its dual statement (obtained by reversing all arrows and reversing the order of all compositions) holds in \(\mathsf{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\).

  • Corollary 1.1.45: A diagram in \(\mathsf{C}\) commutes if and only if the dual diagram in \(\mathsf{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\) commutes: \[h \circ f = g \circ k \quad \text{in } \mathsf{C} \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad f^{\mathrm{op}} \circ h^{\mathrm{op}} = k^{\mathrm{op}} \circ g^{\mathrm{op}} \quad \text{in } \mathsf{C}^{\mathrm{op}}.\]
  • Corollary 1.1.46: A morphism \(f : X \to Y\) is an isomorphism in \(\mathsf{C}\) if and only if \(f^{\mathrm{op}} : Y \to X\) is an isomorphism in \(\mathsf{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\) (with inverse \((f^{-1})^{\mathrm{op}}\)). In particular, \(X \cong Y\) in \(\mathsf{C} \iff X \cong Y\) in \(\mathsf{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\).

10. Complete Exercises & Proofs from Section 1.1

Exercise 1.1.5 (Sets & Relations): Core of a Preorder is an Equivalence Relation

Problem: Let \((X, \lesssim)\) be a preorder. Prove that \(x \sim y \iff (x \lesssim y \text{ and } y \lesssim x)\) is an equivalence relation.

Proof:

  • Reflexivity: Since \(\lesssim\) is reflexive, \(x \lesssim x\) holds for all \(x \in X\). Hence \(x \lesssim x\) and \(x \lesssim x\), so \(x \sim x\).
  • Symmetry: If \(x \sim y\), then \(x \lesssim y\) and \(y \lesssim x\). Since logical conjunction is symmetric, \(y \lesssim x\) and \(x \lesssim y\), meaning \(y \sim x\).
  • Transitivity: Suppose \(x \sim y\) and \(y \sim z\). Then \(x \lesssim y\) and \(y \lesssim z\) (which by transitivity of \(\lesssim\) gives \(x \lesssim z\)), and simultaneously \(z \lesssim y\) and \(y \lesssim x\) (which by transitivity of \(\lesssim\) gives \(z \lesssim x\)). Thus \(x \sim z\). \(\blacksquare\)
Exercise 1.1.12 (Important!): Uniqueness of the Neutral Element in a Monoid/Group

Problem: Prove that if both \(1\) and \(1'\) satisfy the unitality axiom in a monoid \(M\), then \(1 = 1'\).

Proof: Evaluate the product \(1 \cdot 1'\) in two ways:

\[ 1 = 1 \cdot 1' = 1' \]

where the first equality uses that \(1'\) is a right unit (\(g \cdot 1' = g\) with \(g = 1\)), and the second equality uses that \(1\) is a left unit (\(1 \cdot h = h\) with \(h = 1'\)). Categorically, this also proves that the identity morphism \(\mathrm{id}_X\) at any object \(X\) is unique! \(\blacksquare\)

Exercise 1.1.18 (Analysis): Lower Semicontinuous Maps Are Not Closed Under Composition

Problem: Show that there exist two lower semicontinuous (l.s.c.) maps \(f, g : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) such that \(f \circ g\) is not lower semicontinuous.

Counterexample: Recall that \(h : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) is lower semicontinuous iff for every \(\alpha \in \mathbb{R}\), the sublevel/superlevel condition \(\{x : h(x) > \alpha\}\) is open (equivalently \(\liminf_{x \to x_0} h(x) \ge h(x_0)\)).

  • Let \(g(x) = -x\), which is continuous (hence lower semicontinuous).
  • Let \(f(y) = \mathbf{1}_{(0, \infty)}(y)\) (the indicator function of the open ray \((0, \infty)\), which equals \(0\) for \(y \le 0\) and \(1\) for \(y > 0\)). Since \((0, \infty)\) is open, \(f\) is lower semicontinuous. Now perturb or compose with a map that sends \(0\) to a point where \(f\) jumps downward: take \(g(x) = \mathbf{1}_{(0, \infty)}(x)\) (l.s.c., since \(g(0) = 0 \le \liminf_{x \to 0} g(x)\)) and \(f(y) = -y\) (continuous, hence l.s.c.).
  • Then \((f \circ g)(x) = -\mathbf{1}_{(0, \infty)}(x)\), which equals \(0\) at \(x = 0\) and \(-1\) for \(x > 0\). As \(x \to 0^+\), \(\liminf_{x \to 0} (f \circ g)(x) = -1 < 0 = (f \circ g)(0)\), so \(f \circ g\) is upper semicontinuous, not lower semicontinuous! \(\blacksquare\)
Exercise 1.1.19 (Analysis): Fixing Convex and Lower Semicontinuous Maps to Form a Category

Problem: What additional requirement makes convex maps (or lower semicontinuous maps) closed under composition so they form a category?

Solution: Require the maps to be non-decreasing (monotone increasing)!

  • Convex + Non-decreasing: If \(g\) is convex and \(f\) is both convex and non-decreasing, then for \(t \in [0, 1]\): \[ g(tx + (1-t)y) \le t g(x) + (1-t) g(y). \] Applying the non-decreasing map \(f\) preserves the inequality, and convexity of \(f\) gives: \[ f(g(tx + (1-t)y)) \le f(t g(x) + (1-t) g(y)) \le t f(g(x)) + (1-t) f(g(y)). \] Moreover, the composition of two non-decreasing maps is non-decreasing, and \(\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{R}}(x) = x\) is both convex and non-decreasing. Thus non-decreasing convex functions \(\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) do form a category!
  • Lower Semicontinuous + Non-decreasing: Similarly, non-decreasing lower semicontinuous functions \(\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) are closed under composition and include \(\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{R}}\), so they also form a category.
Exercises 1.1.21 & 1.1.22: Small Thin Categories = Preorders; Locally Small 1-Object Categories = Monoids

Exercise 1.1.21: Let \(\mathsf{C}\) be a small category with at most one arrow between any two objects. Then \(X := \mathsf{C}_0\) is a set. Define \(x \lesssim y \iff \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathsf{C}}(x, y) \neq \emptyset\). The existence of \(\mathrm{id}_x : x \to x\) gives reflexivity \(x \lesssim x\), and composition \((y \to z) \circ (x \to y) = (x \to z)\) gives transitivity \(x \lesssim y \land y \lesssim z \implies x \lesssim z\). Hence \((X, \lesssim)\) is a preorder.

Exercise 1.1.22: Let \(\mathsf{C}\) be a locally small category with a single object \(\bullet\). Since \(\mathsf{C}\) is locally small, \(M := \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathsf{C}}(\bullet, \bullet)\) is a honest set. Composition \(\circ : M \times M \to M\) is an associative binary operation on \(M\) with two-sided neutral element \(\mathrm{id}_\bullet \in M\). Thus \((M, \circ, \mathrm{id}_\bullet)\) is a monoid.

Exercises 1.1.26 & 1.1.28 (Important!): Uniqueness of Inverses & Composition of Isomorphisms

Exercise 1.1.26 (Uniqueness of Inverses): Suppose \(g, g' : Y \to X\) are both two-sided inverses of \(f : X \to Y\). Using unitality, associativity, \(f \circ g' = \mathrm{id}_Y\), and \(g \circ f = \mathrm{id}_X\):

\[ g = g \circ \mathrm{id}_Y = g \circ (f \circ g') = (g \circ f) \circ g' = \mathrm{id}_X \circ g' = g'. \]

In particular, viewing a group \(G\) as \(\mathsf{B}G\), inverses in a group are unique.

Exercise 1.1.28 (Composition of Invertible Morphisms): If \(f : X \to Y\) has inverse \(f^{-1} : Y \to X\) and \(h : Y \to Z\) has inverse \(h^{-1} : Z \to Y\), then \((h \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ h^{-1}\), because:

\[ (f^{-1} \circ h^{-1}) \circ (h \circ f) = f^{-1} \circ (h^{-1} \circ h) \circ f = f^{-1} \circ \mathrm{id}_Y \circ f = f^{-1} \circ f = \mathrm{id}_X, \]

and symmetrically \((h \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ h^{-1}) = \mathrm{id}_Z\). (Notice the "shoe-sock" reversal of order!)

Exercises 1.1.34 & 1.1.37: Implicit Morphisms and Commutativity in Diagrams (a)–(h)

See Interactive Lab C above for a visual breakdown of each diagram (a)–(h):

  • Commute automatically: (a) (only \(f\)), (b) (diagonal is already \(g \circ f\)), (f) (\(f\) and \(f^{-1}\) satisfy \(f^{-1} \circ f = \mathrm{id}_X\) and \(f \circ f^{-1} = \mathrm{id}_Y\)), and (g) (single directed edge \(X \xrightarrow{f} X\) between distinct vertices).
  • Require equations to commute: (c) requires \(g \circ f = h\); (d) requires \(g \circ f = \mathrm{id}_X\); (e) requires \(g \circ f = \mathrm{id}_X\) and \(f \circ g = \mathrm{id}_Y\) (which collapses the infinite tower \(f, g, g\circ f, f\circ g, f\circ g\circ f, \dots\)); (h) requires \(h \circ f = g \circ k\).
Exercises 1.1.38 & 1.1.39: Preorder Commutativity & Pasting Two Commutative Squares

Exercise 1.1.38: In a preorder category, between any two objects \(X\) and \(Y\) there is at most one morphism \(X \to Y\). Therefore, any two directed paths starting at \(X\) and ending at \(Y\) must yield morphisms in the singleton set \(\mathrm{Hom}(X, Y)\), so they are automatically equal! Every diagram in a preorder commutes.

Exercise 1.1.39 (Diagram Pasting): Given \(h \circ f = p \circ k\) (left square) and \(\ell \circ g = q \circ h\) (right square), the outer rectangle paths from \(X\) to \(C\) satisfy:

\[ \ell \circ g \circ f = (\ell \circ g) \circ f = (q \circ h) \circ f = q \circ (h \circ f) = q \circ (p \circ k) = q \circ p \circ k. \quad \blacksquare \]

Exercise 1.1.44 & Corollaries 1.1.45–1.1.46: Duality in the Opposite Category C^op

Exercise 1.1.44: Since \(\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathsf{C}^{\mathrm{op}}}(Y, X) = \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathsf{C}}(X, Y)\) with \(f^{\mathrm{op}}\) denoting the exact same underlying element \(f\), \(f = g\) in \(\mathsf{C}\) if and only if \(f^{\mathrm{op}} = g^{\mathrm{op}}\) in \(\mathsf{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\). Thus \(h \circ f = g \circ k \iff (h \circ f)^{\mathrm{op}} = (g \circ k)^{\mathrm{op}} \iff f^{\mathrm{op}} \circ h^{\mathrm{op}} = k^{\mathrm{op}} \circ g^{\mathrm{op}}\), and the inverse of \(f^{\mathrm{op}}\) is \((f^{-1})^{\mathrm{op}}\). \(\blacksquare\)
